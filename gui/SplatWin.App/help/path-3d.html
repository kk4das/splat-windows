The 3D path view

After you analyze a path, 3D view… (in the summary, beside Open the job folder) shows the terrain along the path in 3D: both antennas, the line of sight between them, the Fresnel zone, and the points where SPLAT! finds terrain in the way. It's for seeing what blocks a path and where, which the graphs show only edge-on.

What you see

  • A strip of terrain along the path, about a mile either side (Strip changes it, from ¼ to 5 miles), with a topographic map, a street map, or colors by height on it (Ground; colors by height work without the internet).
  • The antennas as white masts, their tops as yellow dots.
  • The line of sight (light blue) from one antenna top to the other.
  • The Fresnel zone, see-through blue around the line of sight; its lower edge is the dark blue line. Fresnel shows the zone your path analysis used (60 % unless you changed it), the full first zone, or none. Terrain poking into the zone weakens the signal even when the line of sight is clear.
  • The ground right under the path as a yellow line, red where it rises above the line of sight.
  • Red dots: the points SPLAT! lists as obstructions in its report, labeled with the distance from the receiver.
  • At the bottom, one sentence: Blocked (with where, and how high the ground rises above the line of sight), Line of sight clear, but the ground reaches into the Fresnel zone, or Clear. "Blocked" is exactly when SPLAT!'s report lists obstructions.

Viewpoints

  • From receiver (key 1): standing at the receiving antenna, looking at the transmitter. The most useful view: if a hill hides the far antenna, you see the hill, and the far antenna shows only as a white ring through it. A yellow dot inside the ring means you can see it.
  • From transmitter (key 2): the same from the other end.
  • Side (key 3): the whole path from the side, like the Height and Fresnel zone graph.
  • Overview (key 4): from above and behind the receiver.

Drag to turn, scroll to zoom, right-drag to move. Reset view (key R) goes back to the viewpoint you chose. Click the ground to read its distance from the receiver and how far it is above or below the line of sight's height there.

Height exaggeration

Over miles, hills that matter are only tens of feet high, too small to see at true scale. The Height × slider (keys + and −) stretches every height, from 1× (true scale) to 20×; it starts at 3×. The line of sight and the Fresnel zone are stretched the same way, so what is above or below the line of sight stays so at every setting. (The view checks this for every point along the path each time it draws.) For long paths, try 10× or more in the side view.

Where the numbers come from

Nothing in the view is guessed or redrawn by eye. Along the path, the heights are SPLAT!'s own: the same terrain files, the same lookup and the same allowance for Earth's curvature (SPLAT!'s "4/3 Earth", which includes the bending of radio waves in the atmosphere) as SPLAT!'s Height and Fresnel zone graph, and the red dots are SPLAT!'s obstruction list. The app's tests require these to match the files SPLAT! wrote. Either side of the path the same terrain and the same curvature are used. The curvature is taken out of the picture, as in SPLAT!'s graphs, so the line of sight is a straight line.

SPLAT!'s terrain is bare ground. Trees and buildings aren't in it (nor in the view), and on VHF and UHF they matter too. Look at the path in Google Earth, which has 3D buildings and, in many places, trees. If you gave a ground clutter height for the path, SPLAT! raised the ground by it when it looked for obstructions; the view says so and shows the bare ground. User terrain from an obstacle file is shown, as SPLAT! used it.

If the path looks wrong

A station whose position came from a mailing address may not be at its antenna, and a few hundred feet can decide whether a hill is in the way. Check each station's pin is on the antenna itself (Stations), then analyze the path again.

What it needs

The terrain the path was analyzed with. For the sides of the strip the app may need a few more terrain tiles; it downloads them as it does for a path, and if it can't (no internet), it leaves that part of the strip out and says so. The map on the ground needs the internet; without it the ground is colored by height. The view draws with your graphics card (WebGL, part of the WebView2 runtime the map uses).
